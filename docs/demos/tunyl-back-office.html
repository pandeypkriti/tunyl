How it works under the hood

Framework, the Toronto product you tore down this morning, is a document workspace. Upload a PDF, the model reads it, you ask questions and get answers with page citations, and the answers land in registers you can share without a login. This prototype borrows that shape and points it at the paper a subcontractor actually produces.

Step 1
Intake

A photo from the site app. A project inbox address that suppliers and builders email. A data feed from any haulier or supplier that already runs a docket app such as Kynection or Docketbook. Later, a watched SharePoint folder and the purchase orders already in Xero.

Step 2
Reader

One vision-model call per document, into a fixed schema: nine fields for a weighbridge docket, twelve for an invoice. Every field comes back clear, check or unreadable. It never invents a number.

Step 3
Review queue

Records that match the purchase order on every field pass under a rule the office wrote. Everything else waits here, paper and fields side by side, until a person ticks "checked against the paper".

Step 4
Registers

Plain tables. A quantities ledger per project, a waiting-on register, a format map per builder. Ordered, delivered, invoiced, claimed, in the contract's units.

Step 5
Outputs

A Xero sales invoice. The builder's claim spreadsheet. A chaser email. The board view. Nobody outside the office needs a login to receive any of it.

Where Kynection stops, and why that is the gap

Kynection is Autolync Pty Ltd of Seaford, Victoria, trading since 2006, built on the Upvise platform. Its docket is digital from the moment your own driver taps it in: load cycles, weighbridge gross and tare, GPS stamps, a proof-of-delivery photo, then reconciliation against the job and a two-way Xero sync. It is the right answer when you control the truck.

Rob does not control the quarry's weighbridge or the concrete plant's docket book. Nothing on Kynection's site reads a paper or PDF docket from someone else's system, and every flow they describe starts inside their own app. So the two fit together: if a haulier already runs Kynection or Docketbook, take the feed (the fifth item in the review queue). Everything else still arrives on paper and needs the reader.

Their numbers, to borrow with care: paper takes three to five days to process and digital brings ten to fifteen days of cash forward each month. That copy is reused word for word across their blog posts and has no method behind it. The one docket-specific case study is a carpentry contractor whose disputes fell from 15 to 20 percent to under 5. Pricing is not published; a third-party listing quotes about $60 per user per month. The pattern to copy is the pitch, not the numbers: the docket that goes straight from site to claim, and the dispute that never starts because the paper was matched the day it arrived.

Sources: kynection.com.au product pages and case studies, the Xero app store listing, Capterra. Full brief with links in docs/research/kynection-digital-dockets.md.

Three rules the demo is built on

  1. The model never invents a number. A field it cannot read is marked unreadable and left blank. A blank costs ten seconds. A wrong number costs a dispute.
  2. Nothing enters the ledger silently. A record gets in one of two ways: it matches a rule the office wrote (right supplier, product, rate and site, every field read ok), or a person ticks it. The queue holds only the exceptions. That is the answer to "what if the AI gets it wrong", and it is the lesson from Framework's abandoned 2025 email agents.
  3. Nobody outside the office needs a login. The site sends a photo. Suppliers send email. Builders get their own format back. That is Framework's no-login share page, pointed the other way.

What goes into building it

Most of the parts already exist in the hazard engine. The photo-in, structured-record-out shape is the same. What changes is the schema and who ticks the result.

The reader

One prompt per document type, each with three worked examples embedded, the way Kai described in August. Output is JSON against a fixed schema with a state per field. Cents per document at current vision pricing, not dollars.

Intake

The site app from the docket ledger demo. A project email address for supplier invoices and site instructions, which is what Framework's first version did. A watched SharePoint folder later, through Microsoft Graph.

Storage and app

Postgres with four tables: documents, records, ledger lines, waits. Next.js on Vercel, the same setup as the hazard app, so the deploy path is known.

Outputs

CSV first, because it works on day one with every tool they have. Then the Xero API: a sales invoice for claims, bills for matched supplier invoices. Then the builder templates as spreadsheets.

Order of work

  1. Ask Rob's team for twenty real dockets and one supplier invoice. Nothing else matters until this arrives. It also tells you whether they track in tonnes or cubic metres, and which hauliers already run a docket app. Any that run Kynection or Docketbook get a feed, not a camera.
  2. Reader and review queue on those twenty. Count fields right per docket. A clean docket should come back with eight of nine fields clear.
  3. Ledger and a claim export in whatever format their claims already use. Do not design a claim format.
  4. Xero. Sales invoice out, matched bills in.
  5. Builder formats, once they name the platforms.
  6. Waiting on, only if a builder's ops team asks.

How to show it

Rob's committee wants a need, not a tool. Lead with their paper, not with the software.

The walk-through, six moves

  1. Do not open with the screen. Ask who tracks materials today and what they type it into.
  2. Open the review queue on the weighbridge docket. Say: the site photographs it, the office ticks it, nobody types it.
  3. Tick it and approve it. Go to Quantities and claims and show the number land.
  4. Draft the claim. Say: this claim only contains dockets a person has ticked, in your units.
  5. Only then show the overview. That is the "central heart" Rob described, built from the same records.
  6. Builder formats and Waiting on are "we could also" screens. Show them if there is time, or if they raise the pain themselves.

Questions to ask, in their words

  • Who fills the quantities spreadsheet today, and from what piece of paper?
  • Do your dockets come in tonnes or cubic metres, and who does the conversion?
  • Where does the number go next: a claim, Xero, a report to the directors?
  • How many dockets on a busy day, across all the sites? How many of those come from a haulier's app already, and how many on paper?
  • Which builder platforms did you have to feed documents into this year?
  • If the office could tick a docket in ten seconds instead of typing it, who would be doing the ticking?
  • What would make the committee say no?
