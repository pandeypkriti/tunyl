TunylDocket to claim
Prototype for feedback, example data

The site photographs the docket. The office gets the claim.

Paper comes in from suppliers, hauliers and builders you do not control. Tunyl reads it, a rule or a person checks it, and it comes out as a quantities ledger, a progress claim in the builder's format, and one view the directors can read. Nobody outside the office installs anything.

The problem it solves

A civil or earthworks subcontractor moves material by the truckload, hundreds of thousands of cubic metres over a job. Every load arrives with a piece of paper from someone the subcontractor does not control: a weighbridge docket from the quarry, a delivery docket from the concrete plant, a tipping docket from the haulier.

Today a person on site collects the paper and a person in the office types it into a spreadsheet, days later. Nobody matches those dockets against the supplier's invoice until an argument forces it. The progress claim to the builder is built from that spreadsheet, so it goes out late, and the builder's project manager sends it back asking for proof.

Cost one: hours
Typing every docket

One person writes, another types. The office is three to five days behind the site.

Cost two: money
Leaking between docket and invoice

Loads billed with no docket behind them get paid, because nobody had the paper next to the invoice.

Cost three: cash
Claims lodged late

The claim waits for the spreadsheet, then waits again while the builder asks for proof.

The flow in one picture

Three lanes: what the site does, what Tunyl does, what the office does. Money only leaves through the ledger.

The site does one thing
Photograph the docket

Or forward the email. No app, no typing, no login. The paper stays the record.

Tunyl does the middle
Read, match, add up, draft

Fields with a state each. A rule for the clean ones. A running ledger. The claim written from it.

The office does one thing
Tick the exceptions

Seven a week, not two hundred. Paper and fields side by side. Nothing enters silently.

The workflow, in the order it happens

Seven steps. The site does the first two, Tunyl does the middle, the office ticks the exceptions, and the outputs leave from the ledger.

  1. On sitePaper arrivesThe driver hands the docket to your site supervisor at the gate, who signs for the load. Supplier invoices arrive by email. Site instructions come from the builder's site engineer.
  2. On siteSent inThe supervisor photographs the docket, or forwards the email, or the haulier's docket app sends it as data. That is the whole job for the site. On WhatsApp it is one message.
  3. TunylReadThe photo becomes nine fields, each marked read ok, check or unreadable. Nothing is invented. A field that cannot be read stays blank.
  4. TunylMatched or heldIf every field reads ok and the docket matches a purchase order, it goes straight into the ledger under a rule the office wrote. If not, it waits in the review queue. Last week: 205 of 212 passed by rule, 7 waited.
  5. In the officeTickedA person sees the paper and the fields side by side and ticks each exception. Supplier invoices are matched here too, so an invoice billing 26 loads with 24 dockets is held before it reaches ApprovalMax.
  6. TunylLedgerEvery material carries four numbers: ordered, delivered, invoiced, claimed, in the contract's units. The gap between them is the money.
  7. Out the doorClaim, Xero, board viewThe progress claim is drafted from verified dockets only, in the builder's format, with a no-login link where their project manager opens every line to the dockets behind it. Matched invoices go to Xero. The directors get a board view built from the same records, with the payment clock running on each lodged claim.
Who it is for
The subcontractor

    What is not different, said honestly

    Reading a document with a vision model is a commodity. Reconciling dockets to invoices is an old idea. Kynection has a two-way Xero sync today and this prototype does not. The difference is the combination, who it is for, and the fact that it was built from Rob's sentences rather than a feature list.

    Your own edge only counts if it is in the product. The tonnes-to-cubic-metres factor on every docket, the loose-versus-bank note on a tipping docket, the claim in the builder's format, the map to each builder's platform: those are the civil engineering degree and the door-knocking showing up as fields on a screen. Keep adding details like that. They are the moat, not the reader.

    Order of work

    1. Ask Rob's team for twenty real dockets and one supplier invoice. It also tells you whether they track in tonnes or cubic metres, and which hauliers already run a docket app.
    2. Reader and review queue on those twenty. Count fields right per docket. A clean docket should come back with eight of nine fields read ok.
    3. Ledger and a claim export in whatever format their claims already use.
    4. Xero. Sales invoice out, matched bills in.
    5. Builder formats and Waiting on, only when someone asks.

    How to show it

    1. Do not open with the screen. Ask who tracks materials today and what they type it into.
    2. Step 1. Open the quarry docket, send it, watch it pass by rule. Open the handwritten one, send it, watch it wait.
    3. Step 2. Tick the handwritten one. Say: the office ticks seven a week, not two hundred.
    4. Step 3. Draft the claim. Say: only matched or ticked dockets are in it, in your units.
    5. Step 4. Show the board view last. That is the central heart Rob described.
    6. If someone asks "where does that number come from", type the docket number into Ask Tunyl, or open Documents.
    7. Then ask: who fills the spreadsheet today; tonnes or cubic metres; where does the number go next; how many dockets on a busy day and how many already come from a haulier's app; which builder platforms did you feed this year; what would make the committee say no.
